# How Does a Language Model Imagine the News? Outlet-Voice Generation, Measurement Artifacts, and Reward Hacking in Political-Leaning Proxies

*Working paper, draft of 2026-10-17. Authors: Kushaan Vardhan, David Driscoll; analysis pipeline built with Claude Code.
Pre-registration: `docs/protocol_v2.md` (committed before any v2 data, revisions logged). All numbers below are

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly used to simulate people and institutions. Researchers simulate survey
respondents with them ("silicon samples"), newsrooms draft content with them, and readers ask them what a source
would say. If a model is asked to write as *The New York Times* or *Fox News*, what does it produce? Does its
portrayal of each outlet's politics match what the outlet actually publishes?

We study this with Claude Haiku 5.5 writing election-season tweets in the voices of five US news outlets (NYT, Fox
News, Reuters, AP, ABC News) and a neutral "US news organization" voice. We compare the result with 2,500 real tweets
from those outlets' main accounts during the 2024 campaign. A first version of this study (v1) measured
political leaning with an off-the-shelf left/center/right classifier. It reported large differences, some of which
did not survive closer inspection. This paper therefore treats **measurement** as a first-class question. We
ask four things:

1. Is the classifier valid on these texts?
2. Which conclusions hold under a blind LLM judge, a held-out classifier, built counterfactuals and published
   outlet ratings?
3. Do they hold across prompt wordings, generator models, time anchoring, and event-conditioned prompts that fix
   the news being reported?
4. What happens if we *optimise* an open model against the classifier, rewarding it for matching each outlet's real
   label distribution? Does the model become better calibrated to the outlets, or does it learn the classifier's
   shortcuts?

Contributions:

- A pre-registered, multi-measure comparison of LLM outlet portrayals with real outlet output. It includes symmetric
  selection, an event-conditioned arm that holds the news fixed, and information barriers that keep generators blind
  to evaluation criteria and evaluators blind to sources.
- Two measurement artifacts in a widely used political-leaning classifier, each large enough to reverse substantive
  conclusions:
  - a trailing URL changes its label for over a third of tweets;
  - swapping party names in politically neutral text moves its score by tens of points.
- Evidence that, under a validated blind judge, the model's outlet voices differ mainly in agenda and style, not
  framing. Only the Fox News voice carries a framing tilt, and its size matches the real outlet. The model flattens
  the variation of every other outlet toward a uniform neutral register.
- An RL study of a 0.5B open model trained against the classifier. It has format-only, shuffled-target and
  pooled-target controls, and measures transfer to held-out measures and reward-hacking diagnostics [results in
  Section 7].

## 2. Related work

**LLMs as simulators.**
- Argyle et al. (2023) argue that conditioned LLMs can reproduce subgroup response distributions ("algorithmic
  fidelity").
- The cautionary literature is larger:
  - Santurkar et al. (2023) find substantial misalignment between LLM opinions and US demographic groups.
  - Bisbee et al. (2024) find that synthetic survey responses match means but understate variance, and drift with
    prompt wording and over time.
  - Cheng et al. (2023, CoMPosT) formalise caricature as individuation plus exaggeration and find that political
    personas are especially prone to it.
  - Wang et al. (2025) document that LLMs flatten and misportray groups.

  Our design adopts their recommendations: report variance and exaggeration, not only means; test individuation;
  and pre-specify a robustness grid.

**Measuring political slant.** Media-bias classifiers trained on outlet-labelled articles tend to learn outlet
identity (Baly et al. 2020). Sentence-level bias classifiers rely on connotated words and on entity names (Wessel &
Horych 2024). LLMs infer leaning from cues about the source rather than its content (Dai et al. 2025; Park & Kim
2025). Our classifier (arXiv:2507.13913) was trained on articles, posts and LLM-generated statements, and its
authors recommend a politicalness pre-filter.

**LLM-as-judge.** Judges show position and verbosity biases (Zheng et al. 2023) and self-preference that grows with
self-recognition (Panickssery et al. 2024). Their reliability varies by task, and they need validation against
humans (Bavaresco et al. 2025). Design-based supervised learning (Egami et al. 2023) and prediction-powered
inference give valid estimates from LLM labels combined with a random human-coded subset. We use a blinded,
pointwise judge with flip randomisation. We add a second Claude judge and a non-Claude judge, and prepare a
human-coding sample with known inclusion probabilities.

**Reward hacking.** Optimising against a learned proxy improves the proxy while the true objective eventually
degrades. The gap is predictable in KL distance (Gao et al. 2023), and hacking can appear abruptly (Pan et al.
2022). Dr. GRPO (Liu et al. 2025) removes length and standard-deviation normalisation biases from group-relative
policy gradients. We use its advantage form, and we report Goodhart curves against held-out measures.

## 3. Data

### 3.1 Real tweets
The real tweets are 2,500 posts, 500 each from the main X accounts of @nytimes, @FoxNews, @Reuters, @AP and @ABC.
They were drawn by deterministic hashing from the USC 2024 election dataset (arXiv:2411.00376). That
dataset was collected with 44 election keywords, plus "Assassination" from 13 July and "JD Vance" from 15 July 2024.
It therefore samples each outlet's election-related output, not its full feed.

Before analysis we excluded, with pre-registered rules:
- non-English tweets (22);
- replies (25) and thread continuations (9);
- tweets dated before 2024 (7);
- opinion or analysis promotions (68);
- live or video promotions (46);
- within-outlet duplicates (45).

This leaves **2,282** tweets: NYT 417, Fox 467, Reuters 443, AP 479, ABC 476. We assign each to a period: before
Biden's withdrawal on 21 July (P1), 21 July to Election Day (P2), and after Election Day (P3). Quote-led tweets
(292) are flagged, not dropped.

### 3.2 Generated tweets
Each request produced one tweet, with no system prompt, from a prompt giving only the year, a topic and a voice
(prompt v1):

> It is 2024, during the US presidential campaign. Write one tweet that {voice} might post on its main X account about
> {topic}. Reply with only the tweet text: 280 characters or fewer, no links, no hashtags.

**Voices.** The five outlets, each named with its handle, and "a US news organization".

**Topics.** 14, matching the topic taxonomy used on the real tweets. They are worded neutrally; for example,
"abortion" rather than "reproductive rights".

| Condition | Generator | Variation | Tweets |
|---|---|---|---:|
| main-v1 | Haiku 5.5, thinking off | real topic mix, ≥10/topic | 3,150 |
| pilot-v1 | Haiku 5.5 | thinking on vs off | 840 |
| sens-ref | Haiku 5.5 | prompt v1, re-run alongside the variants | 840 |
| sens-t1, t2, t3 | Haiku 5.5 | three reworded templates | 840 each |
| sens-month | Haiku 5.5 | "It is {Month} 2024", months drawn from the real date mix | 840 |
| model-sonnet / model-opus | Sonnet 5.5 / Opus 5.5 | prompt v1 | 840 each |
| sens-baseline | Haiku 5.5 | neutral voice as "no persona" or "a journalist" | 280 |
| five-voice | Haiku 5.5 | all five outlets in one request, order randomised | 700 |
| event-v1 | Haiku 5.5 | same news event for every voice (77 events × 6 voices × 3) | 1,386 |

Sensitivity conditions use 10 tweets per voice × topic. The **event-conditioned arm** works in three steps:
1. Embedding-based clustering of the real tweets (average linkage, at most 3 days apart) proposed 111 candidate
   events covered by at least 3 outlets.
2. Sonnet 5.5, blind to which outlet wrote each tweet, confirmed 77 single events. For each, it wrote a two- or
   three-sentence neutral factual description with no evaluative words, quotations or outlet names.
3. Each voice then wrote a tweet "about this news: {description}", dated to the event.

Real and generated tweets about the same event can then be compared directly.

**Information barriers.**
- No generator ever saw evaluation vocabulary, the rubric, the classifiers or real tweets.
- An automated audit (`study/audit_barriers.py`) checked every one of more than 1,400 distinct prompts.
- Judges saw only masked text: links removed, outlet names, handles, sub-brands and polling partners replaced.

## 4. Measures and validity checks

**M1, the classifier.** `matous-volf/political-leaning-deberta-large` gives P(left/center/right). We report label
shares and the net score 100·[P(right) − P(left)]. All texts are scored with links removed (Section 6.1).

**M2, the blind judge.** Claude Sonnet 5.5, given a codebook and anchored examples, rates:
- **stance** toward Democrats and toward Republicans (−2..+2, or NA if not mentioned);
- **event valence** for each party, separately from framing;
- **slant** on a 7-point scale. The instruction: "judge framing, not the news itself; a factual report of an event
  that is bad for one party is 0 unless word choice, selection, emphasis or omission tilts it";
- **genre**;
- **plausibility** as a real outlet post.

Half of the items, chosen by hash, receive a flipped instrument: the field order is reversed and the scale direction
inverted. Their answers are mapped back afterwards.

**Reliability checks on the judge.**
- **M2b:** Opus 5.5 on the 480-item human set.
- **M2c:** Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, run locally with the same rubric, as a non-Claude judge.
- **Test-retest:** 200 items re-judged in a separate batch.

**M3, held-out classifier.** `premsa/political-bias-prediction-allsides-DeBERTa`. It is used for evaluation only. It
separates Fox from NYT on real tweets (net +7 vs −37), which was the pre-registered condition for using it.

**M3b, loaded wording.** MAGPIE-BABE, a non-directional bias classifier.

**M5b, politicalness.** Political DEBATE, an NLI classifier, scoring "This text is about politics."

**M6, outlet identification.** The judge picks which of the five outlets posted a masked tweet.

**External benchmarks.**
- Audience partisanship of each outlet's domain (Robertson et al. 2018), the primary benchmark.
- AllSides, Ad Fontes, Bakshy et al. (2015) and MBFC as secondary benchmarks.

**Built counterfactuals.** Party-swap pairs (4,148), including 30 built neutral templates with a party slot.

**Human validation set (prepared, not yet coded).** 450 items stratified by source × genre × M1 label × named
party, with recorded inclusion probabilities. It also contains 30 swap pairs and 20 repeats. Two blind coding sheets
and a codebook identical to the judge rubric are provided. **No human labels exist yet. Every judge-based conclusion
is provisional on them.**

## 5. RL study design

Haiku cannot be fine-tuned, so the RL study uses Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct with a LoRA adapter (r = 16, all linear
layers). Its pre-RL outputs are its own baseline. The 0.5B model was selected by a pre-registered feasibility rule:
1.5B ran at 63 s per step on the available Apple M2 Max.

**Prompts.** Prompt v1 for the five outlets. 10 training topics are drawn from each outlet's real topic mix; 4 topics
are held out (immigration, economy, gender rights, environment).

**Reward.**
r(x) = Σ_l P_M1(l|x)·(p_o(l) − q̂_o(l)) − 1[format fail] − β·Σ_t (log π − log π_ref)
- p_o is the outlet's smoothed real M1 label distribution.
- q̂_o is the policy's batch label marginal, shrunk toward its previous value (κ = 16).
- The leaning term rewards labels the policy under-produces relative to the real outlet. Its fixed point is
  q = p, and it is bounded.

**Training.**
- Advantages are group-mean-centred, with groups of 8 completions per prompt and no division by the standard deviation.
- 80 rollouts per step, 300 steps.
- AdamW at learning rate 2e-5.
- β = 0.05, using a sequence-level k1 KL estimate placed in the reward.
- Sampling at temperature 1 with no top-k or top-p truncation.
- fp32 weights. A first bf16 launch produced NaN sampling probabilities and was restarted in fp32 for every
  condition.

**Conditions.**
- calibrated: 3 seeds;
- format-only: 2 seeds;
- shuffled targets, a cyclic derangement of outlets: 1 seed;
- pooled target, with every outlet given the pooled real distribution: 1 seed.

**Evaluation.**
- Every checkpoint and the base model are sampled on training topics, held-out topics, and a reworded template.
- Each sample is scored by M1 (the reward model), M3 and M2, none of which except M1 enter training.
- Reward-hacking diagnostics:
  - M1 with party names masked;
  - party-swap flip rates;
  - party-mention overshoot;
  - distinct-2 and duplicate rates;
  - reference-model NLL;
  - politicalness.

## 6. Statistics

- **Intervals:** a cluster bootstrap with B = 10,000. Near-duplicate clusters are formed by word-3-gram Jaccard
  ≥ 0.5 on masked text, and resampled within strata: voice × requested topic for generated tweets, and
  outlet × predicted topic × named party for real ones.
- **RQ1:** voice-minus-neutral contrasts are standardised to the pooled real topic mix (main-v1), or use equal topic
  weights (variants).
- **RQ2:** generated-versus-real gaps are standardised on predicted topic × named party to each outlet's real mix. We
  report the share of real weight that has no generated support.
- **Decision rules:** pre-registered (protocol §3), with equivalence tests (TOST, ±15 M1 points or ±0.4 judge
  points), Holm correction across 5 voices × 2 measures, and DerSimonian–Laird pooling across the five pre-named
  variants.

## 6. Results I: measurement

### 6.1 A trailing link changes the classifier's answer
Real outlet tweets usually end in a t.co link, and v1 scored them that way. Removing the link from the 2,275 real
tweets that have one changes M1's label for **37.5%** of them. With links, 1.6% of real tweets are labelled "center";
without them, 27.5% are. Appending a synthetic t.co link to generated tweets, which have none, has the same effect:
the label changes for 36.7% of them, and the center share falls from 37.8% to 4.2% (Figure 4).

v1's headline contrast was "1.6% center among real tweets against 39% among generated tweets". That contrast was
therefore a link artifact, not a difference between real and generated writing. All v2 measures score text with
links removed.

### 6.2 The classifier tracks party names; the judge does not
Party-swap counterfactuals (Figure 3) test this directly. On 30 built templates that are neutral by construction
(e.g. "{candidate} will hold a campaign rally in Pennsylvania on Saturday, the campaign said"), swapping Harris for
Trump changes M1's net score by **81 points on average** and flips **47%** of its labels. The judge's slant does not
change for any of them (mean |Δ| = 0.00).

On real and generated tweets that the judge rated neutral (slant 0, neither party favoured), swapping names moves M1
by 45 points (real) and 25 points (generated). The judge moves by 0.17 and 0.03 points on its 7-point scale. These
are judge-selected items, so the judge is favoured here; the templates are the clean test.

On partisan items, a valid measure *should* move when the parties are swapped. There the judge's slant changes for
81% of items (mean |Δ| = 1.53), and M1's change correlates with the judge's (Spearman 0.54).

M1 therefore meets the pre-registered criterion for name sensitivity (H3: mean |Δ| > 20 or flip rate > 15% on neutral
items). The judge does not, so it becomes co-primary.

### 6.3 Judge reliability and agreement among measures
- **Test-retest.** Sonnet's ratings of 200 items re-judged in a separate batch give Krippendorff's α = 0.90 for slant
  and 0.92–0.97 for stances, genre and the NA flags.
- **Second judge.** Opus 5.5 on the 480-item human set agrees with Sonnet at α = 0.80 for slant, 0.84 and 0.78 for the
  stances, and 0.76 for genre. Exact slant agreement is 92%, and the mean difference is −0.01.
- **Order and anchor effects.** Items given the flipped instrument (reversed field order and scale) differ from
  unflipped items by −0.04 ± 0.03 (real) and −0.01 ± 0.01 (generated) in mean slant. Both are negligible.
- **Non-Claude judge.** A local Qwen2.5-7B judge is being run on the same items, with results to follow [pending].
  Both Claude judges come from the generator's model family, so their agreement does not rule out a shared family
  bias. Human coding remains the gate (Section 4).

**Agreement among measures** is modest. On real tweets, M1 correlates with the judge's slant at ρ = 0.39, and M3 at
ρ = 0.23. On event-conditioned generated tweets the correlation with the judge almost vanishes (0.05 and 0.03), even
though M1 and M3 still agree with each other (ρ = 0.54). The two classifiers share signals the judge does not treat as
framing.

**External benchmarks** (n = 5 outlets, descriptive). Ranked by mean judge slant, the real outlets agree with
published outlet ratings, with Spearman ρ of:
- 0.8 against audience partisanship (Robertson et al. 2018);
- 0.9 against AllSides;
- 1.0 against Ad Fontes and Bakshy et al. (2015);
- 0.9 against MBFC.

M1 agrees less often, with ρ of 0.3, 0.4, 0.7, 0.7 and 0.9 against the same five. The judge's ordering of the real
outlets is the more externally valid of the two.

## 7. Results II: how the model imagines each outlet

### 7.1 Agenda and genre differ; framing barely does (RQ1)
Table 1 gives each voice's difference from Haiku's own neutral voice in main-v1, standardised to the real topic mix,
with cluster-bootstrap 95% intervals (B = 10,000).

**Table 1.** Voice minus neutral voice, main-v1 (Haiku 5.5, prompt v1, 525 tweets per voice).

| Voice | M1 net | Judge slant | Stance → Dem | Stance → Rep | M3 net | Names a party (Δ share) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NYT | +2.4 [−4.5, +9.6] | +0.00 [−0.01, +0.02] | −0.01 | +0.01 | +4.4 [+2.2, +6.7] | +0.15 |
| Fox News | **+100.3** [+92.1, +108.0] | **+0.66** [+0.62, +0.70] | **−0.60** | **+0.42** | +52.7 [+46.6, +59.8] | +0.08 |
| Reuters | +41.0 [+34.1, +48.0] | +0.02 [+0.01, +0.03] | −0.02 | +0.02 | +26.3 [+23.1, +29.5] | +0.19 |
| AP | +37.8 [+31.1, +44.8] | +0.02 [+0.01, +0.03] | −0.02 | +0.02 | +26.9 [+24.3, +29.6] | +0.23 |
| ABC News | −29.6 [−35.7, −23.5] | +0.02 [+0.01, +0.03] | −0.02 | +0.02 | −16.6 [−19.3, −13.7] | +0.22 |

*Neutral-voice levels: M1 net −46.2, judge slant −0.02. Stance intervals are within ±0.05 except for Fox.*

**Under the judge, only the Fox News voice frames the news differently.** It is less favourable to Democrats (stance
−0.60) and more favourable to Republicans (+0.42), giving a net slant of +0.66. The other four voices are framed like
the neutral voice. Their slant differences are at most 0.02 points on a 7-point scale, and NYT is *statistically
equivalent* to the neutral voice under TOST at ±0.4 (90% CI [−0.01, +0.02]).

**Both classifiers instead report large, robust differences for Reuters, AP and ABC.** Reuters and AP come out about
40 points to the right of the neutral voice; ABC comes out 30 points to the left.

**The genre decomposition** shows that these classifier differences are not explained by genre mix: genre accounts for
0.4–9 points and within-genre differences for the rest. They arise from what the voices write about and how they word
it, which the judge, focused on framing, does not count as slant.

Agenda differs clearly. Every outlet voice names a party more often than the neutral voice (+8 to +23 points), and
the Fox voice favours certain genres (Section 7.3).

### 7.2 Robustness across prompts, models and time (RQ4)
H1 was pre-registered as robust if a voice-minus-neutral difference keeps its sign with |Δ| ≥ 7.5 (M1) or ≥ 0.2
(judge) in at least 4 of 5 named variants, and a random-effects estimate pooled across them excludes zero (Figure 6).
The variants are three reworded templates, a month-anchored prompt, and Sonnet 5.5 as generator.

- **Fox News: robust on both measures** (5 of 5 variants). Its judge slant ranges from +0.39 (Sonnet) to +1.40
  (template t3).
- **Reuters and AP: robust on M1** (5 of 5) **but not on the judge** (0 of 5).
- **ABC: robust on M1** (4 of 5) **but not on the judge.**
- **NYT: not robust on either measure.**

Equivalence with the neutral voice holds for NYT under both measures in main-v1. Writing all five voices in one
request (exploratory) did not exaggerate the differences: the standard deviation of voice means was 0.36 for slant,
against 0.43 when the voices were written separately. The neutral persona does not explain the neutral voice either:
"no persona" and "a journalist" variants score slant −0.04 and −0.11, against −0.03.

### 7.3 How the portrayals compare with the real outlets (RQ2)
**Table 2.** Real (cleaned) tweets against Haiku's voice (main-v1), unweighted levels.

| Outlet | Judge slant real / generated | Stance → Rep real / generated | Names a party real / generated | Passes the dataset's keyword filter, generated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NYT | **−0.24** / −0.01 | −0.29 / −0.01 | 97% / 29% | 21% |
| Fox News | **+0.71** / +0.64 | +0.26 / **+0.49** | 96% / 21% | 20% |
| Reuters | −0.05 / +0.00 | −0.17 / +0.00 | 98% / 32% | 29% |
| AP | −0.08 / +0.00 | −0.11 / +0.00 | 93% / 37% | 38% |
| ABC News | −0.07 / −0.00 | −0.13 / +0.00 | 98% / 35% | 21% |

Four findings stand out.

1. **Fox News: the size of its framing is right, its tone is not.** Haiku's Fox voice matches real Fox's mean slant
   (+0.64 against +0.71), with an exaggeration ratio of 1.02. Its spread across tweets also matches (SD ratio 1.07).
   But it is warmer toward Republicans than real Fox (+0.49 against +0.26).
2. **Haiku erases the NYT's left tilt.** Real NYT tweets lean slightly left under the judge (slant −0.24; stance toward
   Republicans −0.29). The generated NYT voice is neutral.
3. **Haiku flattens every outlet except Fox.** Within each of the other voices, the spread of slant is 0–20% of the
   real outlet's (SD ratios 0.20 for NYT, 0.09 Reuters, 0.00 AP, 0.09 ABC; Figure 5). Real outlets publish a range
   of framings, and the model writes a single register.
4. **The genre gap dominates everything else.**
   - **Real outlet tweets** are mostly news reports (52–71%), with breaking news, polls and quote-led items making up
     much of the rest. Under 3% are voting reminders.
   - **The topic-only voices** are mostly issue explainers (33–54%). Exactly 23% of every voice are voting reminders,
     which matches the share of prompts that asked about "voting and the election". That topic wording therefore
     produced the voting reminders itself.
   - **The Fox voice** writes no news reports at all: 43% are TV-promotion lines ("Tune in tonight…") and 29% opinion.
     Real Fox tweets are 52% news reports and 33% quote-led.
   - **As a result,** generated tweets rarely name a party (21–37%, against 93–98% of real tweets), and only 20–38%
     would have passed the source dataset's own election-keyword filter.

**Can generated tweets be told from real ones?** Yes, almost perfectly when they are written from a topic alone. A
logistic classifier on sentence embeddings, cross-validated by near-duplicate cluster, separates them with AUC ≈ 1.00
for every outlet. Given the same events, the outlets are harder to tell apart (AUC 0.79–0.98).

The blind judge also identifies the outlet behind real tweets more often (67% against 20% chance) than behind
topic-only generated tweets (52%) or event-conditioned ones (37%). Generated voices carry *less* outlet-specific
signal than the real accounts, except for Fox (71% vs 92%) and ABC (71% vs 61%). ABC's distinctiveness is a
stylistic template, not a framing.

**The standardised comparison is only partial.** It matches real and generated tweets on predicted topic × named
party. But 73–79% of each outlet's real weight falls in cells with no generated tweets, mostly because real tweets
almost always name a party. Under the pre-registered rule (more than 20% of real weight unsupported), RQ2's
standardised comparison is therefore "partial", and we rely on the event arm.

**When the news is held fixed (event-conditioned arm), the voices converge.** Given the same neutral event
description, every voice writes a news report (78–85%), and voice-minus-neutral slant is small. Fox is the largest at
+0.11 [0.02, 0.21]; the others are +0.03 to +0.05 with intervals including zero.

Against each outlet's real tweets on the same events, the gaps are small and none is distinguishable from zero.
- **Fox:** the generated voice is less right-leaning than real Fox (−0.19 [−0.46, +0.10]), because on these events
  real Fox scores +0.32 and the voice +0.13.
- **NYT:** close to real NYT (+0.09 [−0.01, +0.18]).

**H2 is not supported.** The v1 claim that Haiku portrays ABC far to the left of the real ABC rests entirely on M1.
- **Under M1**, the keyword-filtered standardised gap is −75 points [−95, −57].
- **Under the judge**, the same gap is +0.11 [−0.02, +0.24], and +0.05 [−0.02, +0.13] in the event arm.

The M1 gap reflects the ABC voice's template of issue explainers and voting reminders, which M1 labels "left". It
does not reflect a left framing.

### 7.4 Word-level differences (exploratory)
Log-odds with an informative Dirichlet prior make the genre gap concrete:
- **Generated voices overuse** second-person and civic words ("your", "you", "voters", "where … stand", "candidates",
  "check", "polling", "know") and abstract issue nouns ("economy", "abortion", "immigration", "inflation", "border").
- **Real outlets overuse** people and events: "Biden", "Trump", "Harris", "president", "former", "says", "after",
  "GOP", "House".
